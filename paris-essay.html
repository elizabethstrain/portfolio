Notes from a Summer in Paris

On art de vivre, and what the French understand about brands that most marketers never will.
By Elizabeth Strain

I went to Paris the summer I was twenty-something with an internship at a family-owned natural wine company and a quiet suspicion that I was about to learn something I couldn't get in a classroom.

I was right. I just didn't expect the lesson to be about marketing.

The French have a phrase: art de vivre. The art of living. It sounds like a lifestyle concept, something you'd see on a mood board or a brand deck. But in Paris it isn't a concept at all. It's just Tuesday. It's the baker who has been making the same croissant for thirty years because he has no interest in making a different one. It's the restaurant that doesn't take reservations not because it's trendy but because it never occurred to them to change. It's the wine on the table at lunch, the patio that spills onto the street, the unhurried conversation that stretches past dark.

Nobody is performing any of it. That's the thing.

Working inside a small, family-led wine company that summer, I watched how they talked about their product. Or more accurately, how they didn't. There were no talking points. No brand pillars. No carefully crafted elevator pitch. There was just genuine pride in what they made and a quiet assumption that if you tasted it you would understand. The story wasn't separate from the thing. It was the thing.

Every corner of that city operated the same way. The boulangerie with the line out the door at 7am. The fromagerie where the owner knew every wheel by name. The architecture that didn't ask to be noticed but stopped you cold anyway. Paris is full of things that have never once tried to go viral and have been beloved for centuries.

I think about that a lot now.

The brands I'm most drawn to work this way. They don't explain themselves. They don't chase attention. They build something so considered, so genuinely good, that the story takes care of itself. They understand that the most powerful thing a brand can do is make people feel something real. Not manufacture a feeling and push it out into the world, but create the conditions for it to happen naturally.

Art de vivre isn't a marketing strategy. But it might be the best one I've ever seen.
